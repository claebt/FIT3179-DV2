Why We Love the Coast

A data story about what makes a great Australian beach, and how to find yours.

Australia's beaches are a national obsession. This story looks at their scale, how often we use them, what's on offer at them, and how to enjoy them safely.

In this story

    1A coastline like no other

    Western Australia alone has more coastline than most countries. Australia's coastline is spread very unevenly: Western Australia, Queensland and the Northern Territory hold most of it, while New South Wales, the most populous state, has one of the shortest.

    How to read: darker blue means more coastline.

    Western Australia's 20,788 km is about 35% of the national total, and almost 40% of Australia's coastline is around islands. The numbered circles are Tourism Australia's ten best beaches for 2026 — though Ellery Creek Big Hole sits inland, nowhere near the coast.

    Try it: Hover over a beach in the list to read more about it.

    2Where we go and what we do

    The coast is part of everyday Australian life. 87% of Australians live within 50 km of the coast, and more than 16 million adults made over 630 million coastal visits in a single year. For most Australians, the coast means the beach: 88 of every 100 coastal visits are to a beach, which is more than 550 million visits in a year.

    How to read: each square is 1% of coastal visits, or more than 6 million. Sand-coloured squares are visits to a beach, and grey squares are visits to anywhere else on the coast.

    Swimming is by far the most popular coastal activity: 57% of coastal visitors swim or wade, but most of them go less than once a month. Surfing and rock fishing are the only activities where regulars outnumber occasional participants.

    How to read: activity names run down the middle. Bars to the right are people who go at least once a month, and bars to the left go less often. The bracket marks the two activities where the right bar is longer.

    3What's at the beach

    Facilities vary enormously from beach to beach. Beach-by-beach data isn't publicly available nationally, so from here we zoom into Victoria, which publishes open data on every ocean beach. The 13 facilities tracked range from the basic, like toilets and drinking water, to the comparatively rare, like showers and swimming pools.

    Try it: Drag the two handles in the slider below the map to show only beaches with that many facilities.

    Each of Victoria's 734 ocean beaches is a dot, coloured by how many of the 13 recorded facilities it has. Most of Victoria's coast has none at all, and the darkest dots, beaches with 10 or more facilities, are few (29 of 734) and cluster around the bays near Melbourne.

    How to read: darker brown means more facilities. Hover over a dot for the beach's name and how many facilities it has.

    Almost three in four beaches have no facilities at all. Grouping the 734 beaches by how many facilities they have shows 535 (73%) with none, and just 29 (4%) with ten or more. Most of the rest have only one to six facilities, and just 64 beaches have seven or more.

    How to read: each dot's distance from zero is the number of beaches in that band, in the same colours as the map.

    Facilities tend to come as a package. If a beach has one facility, this shows how likely it is to have each of the others: every beach with a pool also has toilets, water and a kiosk, but just 8% of beaches with toilets have a pool.

    How to read: pick a row, then read across. Darker cells means how likely the row's facility also has the column's.

    4What the beach is like

    What makes a beach hazardous? Facilities are only half the picture. The same Victorian survey also scored every ocean beach from 0 to 10 for physical hazards that could affect swimming safety. Do well-equipped beaches tend to be safer? Only partly: 38% of beaches with no facilities are rated 7–10, against 9% of those with four or five, and none with ten or more.

    How to read: each bar is the group of beaches with that many facilities. Darker orange means a higher hazard rating.

    Which physical features go with higher hazard? For each hazard rating, this shows the share of beaches that have each feature recorded in the survey, such as rocks, reefs, headlands, sharks and winds. Reefs, rocks and headlands generally grow more common as the rating rises, while structures go the other way: 62% at rating 1, but 4% or fewer from rating 4 up.

    How to read: darker cells mean more of the beaches at that rating have the feature. Blank cells mean no beaches at that rating have the feature.

    5Is the water clean?

    Ocean beaches nearly always pass, but sheltered waters don't: 97% of monitored NSW ocean beaches were Good or Very Good in 2026, against 79% of all swim sites, because estuaries, lakes, lagoons and rivers flush more slowly after rain. Each circle on the map is one swim site, and a bigger circle means more enterococci, a bacteria that signals faecal contamination.

    How to read: blue means a better grade and orange a worse one, so a large orange circle is a site that is both graded poorly and has high bacteria counts. Colour is the site's long-term annual grade, so the two can differ.

    Try it: Click a grade or enterococci level in the legends below to filter only those sites. Click off the legend to reset.

    Grades differ across regions. Beach water quality is graded from Very Good to Very Poor, and each bar shows the share of a region's graded swim sites at each grade. Shoalhaven Coast and Port Stephens have only Very Good sites, while Western Sydney has none, and most of its sites are Poor or Very Poor. Only Port Hacking, Botany Bay and Western Sydney have Very Poor sites.

    How to read: each bar adds up to 100% of that region's graded sites. Regions at the top have the highest share of Good or Very Good sites, and regions at the bottom have the lowest.

    Linked to the map: Click a grade in the map's legend and that grade stays solid here.

    Water quality improved in every group shown, but estuaries still lag. Between the 2025 and 2026 reports, the share of NSW swim sites graded Good or Very Good rose from 95% to 97% for ocean beaches, from 87% to 97% for Sydney's ocean beaches, from 78% to 81% for Sydney Harbour, from 76% to 79% for all swim sites, and from 63% to 69% for estuarine sites.

    How to read: each pair of dots joins one group's 2025 and 2026 results, and the label on the right is the change in percentage points. Groups overlap, since Sydney's ocean beaches are also part of all ocean beaches.

    6Enjoying it safely

    Every coastal drowning last year happened away from the flags and patrols. Last year 154 people drowned on Australia's coast, the highest number on record. The national report found none of the 154 deaths happened between the red and yellow flags while a patrol was on duty, and nearly three in four (73%) were over 1 km from a Surf Life Saving service. Lifesavers patrol only 6% of beaches in peak summer, and 1% year-round. Each state is resized to match its deaths, so a state with twice the deaths covers twice the area, and colour shows the rate per 100,000 people.

    How to read: small states have fewer deaths partly because fewer people live there, so compare colours for risk, not sizes. States are moved closer together to save space. Hover for exact figures.

    A high rate can come from small numbers. This funnel plot compares each state with the national rate of 0.58 deaths per 100,000 people, and states outside the shaded funnel differ by more than chance. Victoria's 20 deaths are about half the 40 expected, and Western Australia's 27 are above the 17 expected. South Australia, with the highest rate, falls just inside the funnel, as do Tasmania and the Northern Territory, whose rates rest on only five and one deaths. New South Wales and Queensland, the two largest states for deaths, both sit close to the national rate.

    How to read: each dot is a state, and the dashed line is the national rate. Dots outside the inner band differ from it by more than chance would usually allow, and the outer band is stricter.

    7What to look for in a beach

    A great beach is more than a beautiful one. Back to the question this story started with: what makes a great Australian beach, and how do you find yours? The data points to four things worth checking before you go.

    So pick a beach with the facilities you need, check its conditions and water quality before you go, and swim between the flags.

    1. Facilities Almost three in four Victorian beaches have none, so check for toilets and water first.
    2. Conditions Victorian beaches carry a hazard rating for physical features that could affect swimming. Check it so you know what you're facing.
    3. Water Rain is the main cause of beach pollution, so check your state's water-quality forecast before you swim.
    4. Flags Swim between the red and yellow flags. Last year's coastal drownings all happened outside them, outside patrol times, or at unpatrolled locations.
